# Concordant Temples

## Identity

The Concordant Temples are an old and internally diverse federation of major
religious authorities associated with former Meridian cultures and several
successor societies. Their traditions disagree about creation, divine
personhood, revelation, death, and the relationship between mind and matter.
They are concordant in the narrower historical sense that they recognize one
another's sanctuaries, burial protections, and authority to refuse certain
forms of technical desecration.

The federation is not one church, one doctrine, or one government. Its precise
membership, founding period, internal hierarchy, and present geographic reach
remain unresolved. Its age is nevertheless sufficient for temple inventories,
prohibitions, and copied fragments to contribute to the early documentary
history of the Rzez.

## The Severed God prohibition

The Temples are the authorities that prohibited the outlawed Severed God
doctrine. That doctrine holds that material reality remains coherent because
every fragment remembers a murdered divine whole and that the Rzez attacks
Creation's act of self-recognition.

The prohibition does not establish a single approved alternative. Some Temples
reject the doctrine as blasphemy. Others reject it because it converts a
technical instrument into proof about divine reality. Still others hold that a
theology which makes cosmic unity technically vulnerable gives worshippers a
reason to seek the very equations the religion condemns.

Possession of a complete Severed God text was once punishable by death in at
least one Concordant jurisdiction. Present Temples disagree about whether that
sentence was ever just, whether any “complete” text existed, and whether later
fragments were expanded by Rzez cults after Valec.

## Blackglass as burdened witness

The Temples' most influential shared position treats the
[Blackglass Compendium](../../frame/blackglass-tradition.md) as a **burdened
witness**. It may preserve testimony necessary to recognize danger, honor the
dead, and prevent repetition. It may not be treated as a private revelation
whose truth grants the reader permission to act.

This produces a practice that outsiders often call hypocrisy. Temples condemn
lay reading and public recitation while maintaining sealed fragments, trained
custodians, and defensive concordances. Temple authorities answer that a witness
kept under obligation is not the same thing as an oracle placed upon an altar.

The practical rules vary, but widely recognized forms include:

- no lone reader for a passage with constructive content;
- no complete translation key stored beside the technical body it unlocks;
- no ritual recitation of equations, synchronization counts, or complete
  geometric sequences;
- preservation of victims' testimony even when constructive parameters are
  removed;
- independent readers assigned to identify theological metaphor, technical
  instruction, and later interpolation; and
- refusal to confirm an existential claim through reconstruction.

These practices are religious obligations rather than proof that Blackglass is
supernaturally active. They overlap with secular information control because
both traditions learned from some of the same disasters.

## The moral limit of comprehension

Concordant teachers commonly argue that a reader's willingness to bear personal
risk does not authorize risk to everyone else. Understanding can therefore be a
moral act with external consequences. A person who completes a reconstructable
principle has changed the world even before building anything, because the
principle can now be remembered, coerced, copied, stolen, or worshipped.

This does not make curiosity sinful in itself. Temple schools include historians,
physicians, engineers, and legal witnesses. Their boundary is that no desire for
certainty justifies making an irreversible event the test of whether a warning
was correct.

A maxim found in several Temple families reads:

> *There are truths one may carry, truths one may speak, and truths for which no
> listener can consent.*

## Rites of incompletion

Several later religious practices are associated with Blackglass taboo, although
no one rite belongs to the entire federation.

Some funerary readings leave one sentence unfinished, marking the dead as a life
that cannot be reduced to a completed account. Some temples cover polished dark
surfaces during examination of a prohibited fragment. Others maintain an open
door, visible heat path, unsynchronized lamp, or dissenting reader so that the
act of study does not symbolically reproduce closure, unanimity, or perfect
continuity.

The rites borrow cultural language from the forbidden systems without asserting
shared physics. An open door does not defeat a Rzez. An uneven prayer does not
break The Agreement. A cooling stone does not free a Closed Night. The symbols
state that worship should remember the rights those systems violated.

## Conflict with devotional movements

The Temples condemn [Blackglass devotional movements](blackglass-devotional-movements.md)
for turning danger into authority. The principal offense is not simply that a
cult reads prohibited text. It is that the cult treats the trouble following a
fragment as evidence that the fragment is sacred, thereby making every casualty
an argument for further exposure.

Temple responses range from pastoral recovery and purchase of fragments to
cooperation with courts, public denunciation, and destruction of immediately
reconstructable material. They do not agree about whether a corrupted or
incomplete fragment should be preserved. One school holds that even a false
passage must remain available as evidence of the people it misled. Another holds
that some errors are loaded instruments and that copying their history repeats
their operation.

The Houses of the Lower State and Rzez liberation cults are opposed on their own
terms. The Temples do not claim that worship of The Rehearsal, the Rzez, and the
Compendium share one mechanism or one heresy merely because their adherents
borrow from one another.

## Deliberately unresolved

- when and where the first concordance among Temples was made;
- how many religious families and jurisdictions currently belong;
- which Temple inventories are independent witnesses rather than copies of one
  lost source;
- whether any Temple possesses a substantially more complete Blackglass
  recension than it admits;
- whether the death penalty for the Severed God text was exceptional or common;
- how Temple custodians are selected, trained, and released from service; and
- whether any devotional movement began inside a sanctioned custodial order.
