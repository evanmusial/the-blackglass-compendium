# Editorial notes: Collection Identity and Names

These are the retained authorial notes for [Collection Identity and Names](../../frame/collection-identity.md).
The source metadata and lifecycle status remain unchanged. Original guidance,
research references, and reservations are preserved below; relative links are
rebased to this companion location.

## Naming rules

- Use *The Codex of Arcane Engineering* for the formal in-world corpus and entry
  masthead.
- Use *The Blackglass Compendium* for the project and this particular recension.
- Do not call every source fragment “blackglass.”
- Do not define blackglass as one universal magical substance or treat every
  dark archival material as part of the textual lineage.
- An official character may deny that the Blackglass recension exists while
  still recognizing the Codex.
- “Where Blackglass goes, trouble follows” is an established cultural axiom, not
  authorial proof that the collection is conscious or supernatural.

## Reserved authorial meaning

The first meaning of **Blackglass** is known authorially but must remain unknown
to the represented reader, the present compilers, and the cultures described in
the current collection. The material, redaction, and mirror explanations are
sincere later explanations for an inherited term whose original grammar and
use have been lost. They may preserve echoes of the truth, but none is the
source of the name.

**Blackglass originally names a civilizational condition or diagnosis, not a
material, object, archive, or book. A civilization becomes Blackglass when it
possesses reproducible knowledge of its own destruction and can no longer
contain, forget, or render that knowledge harmless.**

In the closest available metaphor, knowledge is glass because it permits a
civilization to see. It becomes black when it ceases to be a window onto the
world and reflects only the civilization's own capacity to destroy itself. This
metaphor explains the English compound for authorial purposes; it does not
establish the words or etymology of any lost source language.

The work now called *The Blackglass Compendium* inherited its title through
semantic drift. Its earliest sense was closer to **the collected evidence that
we have become Blackglass** than to the name of a particular volume. The
Compendium is therefore not dangerous because it is made from black glass or
because one supernatural force inhabits it. It is the surviving case record of
civilizations crossing a threshold after which their most destructive knowledge
cannot reliably be made secret again.

This meaning also reverses the apparent causality of the warning “where
Blackglass goes, trouble follows.” The arrival of a fragment need not cause the
trouble. Recognizable Blackglass is evidence that the underlying process is
already under way. A later collection may eventually recover a more direct
usage whose force is simply: **this world is now Blackglass.**

## Reveal boundary

Do not reveal or confirm this meaning in the present collection. It is reserved
for a future collection and should emerge through accumulated philological and
historical pressure rather than a single explanatory disclosure. Permissible
early clues include uses that predate vitreous media, grammar inconsistent with
a material noun or book title, and passages implying that a place or
civilization *became* Blackglass. Each clue must remain compatible with a less
conclusive in-world interpretation until the later reveal.

The reserved meaning does not join the forbidden systems under one mechanism,
make the Compendium conscious, or prove that its fragments cause catastrophe.
It is a diagnosis of accumulated technical knowledge and civilizational risk,
not a seventh technology or universal force.
