# The Blackglass Tradition

## The one certainty

The origin, age, first medium, original language, and first archivists of the
Blackglass Compendium cannot be established. Its oldest recoverable textual
layers are already translations. Its apparent table of contents refers to books
for which no authenticated body text survives. Recent recensions contain events
that occurred long after the earliest surviving leaves were copied. No known
editor possessed an original against which the work could be made whole.

One statement nevertheless appears with unusual consistency in formal cautions,
religious prohibitions, expedition briefings, survivor speech, and popular
folklore:

> **One thing is certain: where Blackglass goes, trouble follows.**

The restrained institutional form is less comforting:

> No causal relationship has been established. The association is uncontested.

The distinction matters. Blackglass may cause disaster, reveal a danger already
present, attract people prepared to create one, or be carried afterward by the
survivors who need language for what occurred. Some incidents begin before a
fragment is found. Others begin when a previously harmless fragment is
translated correctly. In several famous cases the order cannot be recovered.

Blackglass scholarship therefore refuses both easy superstition and easy
reassurance. The collection is not authorially established as a conscious,
supernatural, or self-propagating object. Civilizations have still accumulated
enough experience to fear it as if it were all three.

## A lineage rather than one book

The formal *Codex of Arcane Engineering* is the larger institutional corpus of
technical history, defensive reference, jurisprudence, and controlled evidence.
The **Blackglass Compendium** is the later, reconstructed, expanded, and probably
unauthorized recension represented here. It includes material excluded from
safer Codex editions and preserves relationships that official filing systems
separate.

There is no single Blackglass volume from which all later copies descend. The
name applies to a recognizable editorial lineage distributed among manuscripts,
plates, data stores, quotations, legal exhibits, field transcriptions, and
memory traditions. A complete edition is an editorial claim, not an object that
has been demonstrated to exist.

This lets the current recension be both ancient and contemporary. Its oldest
structure, inherited cautions, unidentified hands, and fragments are older than
every surviving state. Its later annotations can discuss Valec, Ninth
Holding, Nacre Hall, Apsis, and current rumors. The Compendium is an old vessel
whose cargo has never stopped changing.

Official collections usually preserve entries. Blackglass preserves the wounds
between them: removed cross-references, incompatible names, censor orders,
survivor objections, suspicious omissions, and evidence that an industrial
practice in one age became a prohibited instrument in another. The danger of
the recension is often not one fact but the act of collation.

> The secret is not that the facts exist. The secret is that they belong
> together.

## Why the glass is black

Three explanations for the name remain in circulation. They are not mutually
exclusive.

### Archival material

Several early witness families include dark vitreous leaves, ceramic-glass
laminates, and carbon-rich optical wafers. Some retain information after burial,
vacuum exposure, fire, chemical attack, or deliberate surface damage. None is
indestructible. Surviving pieces are crazed, delaminated, incomplete, or readable
only through imaging that consumes part of the remaining signal.

No one material is established as the original blackglass. Different artifacts
called by the name use different local technologies. The apparent durability of
the tradition comes from repeated copying, not from an unlimited substance.

### Redaction

On several reflective witness media, overwritten, ablated, or optically flooded
regions appear as smooth dark fields. The underlying text cannot be read
directly, but line length, indexing, quotations, and correction traces sometimes
permit limited reconstruction. Early scholars describe such work as reading
“behind the black glass.”

The phrase later expands to include any omission whose shape remains visible:
the censored cross-reference, the missing name, the equation whose dependent
tables survived, or the warning copied after its reason was removed.

### Mirror

The later philosophical reading treats forbidden technology as a dark mirror.
The reader expects an alien obscenity and instead finds recognizable engineers,
careful budgets, lawful procurement, sincere safety arguments, frightened
workers, useful civil ancestors, and institutions that believed they could stop
one step before catastrophe.

In this reading the glass is black because it returns no comforting image. What
it reflects is civilization's capacity to understand exactly enough.

## The scattered leaves

No explored region is entirely free of Blackglass claims. Every system subjected
to sustained survey has produced at least one alleged fragment, quotation,
notation family, or physical correspondence. Many are frauds, dependent copies,
misidentified local records, or later texts planted in older contexts. Enough
independent finds survive authentication that universal dismissal is no longer a
serious position.

The fragments are rarely grand discoveries. They are single leaves, partial
indices, a warning without its subject, the lower half of a diagram, a list of
unfamiliar names, or a paragraph already known from another recension except for
one consequential word. They appear without the orderly context expected of an
archive.

This distribution is commonly called the **scattered-leaf problem**. Navigators
give it a less formal description:

> Every new place yields a horizon, a grave no one admits to digging, and a
> page of Blackglass.

### Accounts in stone

Blackglass passages have been translated from boundary stones, quarry faces,
burial chambers, foundation courses, cave walls, road markers, and the reused
backs of civic inscriptions. Some are plainly local copies made by people with
limited media. Others occur in places whose builders left no recognized
technical tradition capable of explaining the content.

Stone versions often preserve cautions better than equations. A carving may
accurately warn against completing a particular geometry while rendering every
technical noun as a religious image. Another may preserve a sequence of
operations but translate the reason for them as a genealogy, pilgrimage, or
funerary rite. The current recension treats such accounts as evidence of
transmission, not automatic evidence that the carvers understood or used the
weapon described.

### Pages in open space

Individual leaves have also been recovered in open space: thin printed foils,
sealed fiber sheets, ceramic slips, optical tiles, and data wafers moving without
an associated vessel. Some occupy stable or slowly decaying orbits. Others are
found on transfer paths that do not lead cleanly to any known launch site.

Most can be explained as wreckage, jettisoned contraband, funerary deposits, or
the remains of courier capsules whose less durable structures disappeared. A few
cannot. Their surfaces show no mounting scars, their trajectories have no
accepted parent event, or their text belongs to a local language whose users
deny ever producing a Blackglass copy.

The famous phrase **a page without a book** originally described these finds. It
now means evidence whose existence is clear but whose chain of custody begins
only at discovery.

### The proximate-medium problem

Blackglass fragments tend to use a medium plausible for the technological
environment in which they are found. Stone-cutting cultures find incisions.
Ceramic industries find fired tablets. Metallurgical societies find foil or
etched plate. Mature archival cultures find optical leaves, molecular stores,
or ordinary copied files. Spacefaring societies find pages that can survive the
local vacuum and radiation environment.

This **proximate-medium pattern** is not perfect, and no mechanism is accepted.
Ordinary copying explains much of it: people reproduce a dangerous text using
what they have. Fraud explains more. Cultural selection explains why familiar
objects are recognized and preserved while unfamiliar debris is discarded.

The remaining cases are troubling because the fragment appears older than the
local copying tradition, because no source copy is known, or because the local
medium contains an error associated with a distant recension that contact
records say it could not have received. The pattern does not prove that
Blackglass manufactures or adapts its own carriers. It prevents scholars from
comfortably ruling that possibility out.

## Landscape correspondences

The most disputed exterior witnesses are not texts at all.

Planetary exploration has recorded ridgelines, crater chains, drainage basins,
mineral boundaries, void networks, and continental fracture patterns resembling
the characteristic geometry of instruments described in the Compendium. Some
are vague resemblances publicized after the fact. Higher-resolution surveys often
reduce them to ordinary geology.

In other cases, additional resolution strengthens the correspondence. A ridge
becomes a sequence of separated anchor sites. A basin contains regular absences
at the positions occupied by a known field diagram. A moon's interior resembles
the residual pattern expected after planetary characterization. A set of
apparently natural promontories closes the same incomplete surface that a
radiant-return analysis would forbid completing.

Several such worlds are uninhabited and have no evidence of prior visitation.
Some formations are physically dated to intervals earlier than the known
civilizations that possess the corresponding descriptions. Claims that an
entire landform is a written or engineered Blackglass passage remain weak unless
material sequence, artificial regularity, or independent records support them.
The repeated appearance of the problem is established; any one interpretation
is not.

The contradiction is irreducible. If the landscapes are natural, intelligence
may repeatedly mistake the same shapes for weapons. If they are artificial, the
known history of exploration is false or radically incomplete. If the
Compendium merely predicts forms that nature can produce, its oldest descriptions
may be classifications rather than inventions. If some other relationship
exists, no current theory states it without outrunning the evidence.

The Blackglass editors call these **landscape correspondences**, not deployments.
Expedition crews use a blunter term: **the weapons waiting outside**.

## Translation as succession and exposure

The Compendium has crossed hundreds of translations and recension families.
This is not one clean chain in which each language replaced the last. Copies
branched, recombined, borrowed annotations from rivals, and sometimes returned a
passage to a language related to one in which an older form had already existed.

Languages die. Units lose their reference standards. Technical verbs become
metaphors. A legal prohibition survives after the prohibited apparatus is
forgotten. A prayer preserves a sequence more accurately than a damaged field
manual. Translation is therefore necessary to keep the warnings alive.

It is also an exposure event.

Reading can remain superficial. Translation requires decisions about what a
sentence means, which distinctions the destination language lacks, and whether
an apparent error is corruption or containment. A translator may become the
first person in centuries to understand that three harmless paragraphs compose
one operational principle.

Some jurisdictions commission translations only when an older language is near
loss. Work is divided among readers who do not receive adjacent sections.
Technical terms are carried forward untranslated. Units are left deliberately
uncorrected. Independent teams produce incompatible renderings so that no one
copy becomes both fluent and reconstructable. These practices preserve safety at
the cost of fidelity and preserve fidelity at the cost of safety.

> A translation is not a copy. It is an experiment performed upon meaning.

## Knowledge as capability

Blackglass information is not uniformly hazardous, and no one cognitive or
physical mechanism links every entry. Its dangers recur through several
different paths:

- **Instructional danger:** a description supplies equations, geometry,
  materials, timing, or operational sequence sufficient for construction.
- **Inferential danger:** separately harmless facts become a design when placed
  beside one another.
- **Recognitional danger:** a reader learns how to identify, approach, activate,
  or misuse an artifact that was already present.
- **Constitutive danger:** for a limited class of systems, modeling,
  synchronization, reciprocal observation, or high-fidelity rehearsal can form
  part of the dangerous architecture rather than merely describe it.
- **False-completion danger:** an incomplete or mistranslated account inspires an
  attempted reconstruction whose failure mode is catastrophic even though the
  original claim was wrong.
- **Institutional danger:** secrecy, accusation, and preventive isolation destroy
  trust and infrastructure before any weapon is confirmed.

The categories do not make all knowledge magical or all study illegal. They
explain why an unread volume can be inert while three remembered sentences in a
qualified mind become contraband. An uncomprehending courier may be safer than a
careful engineer.

The Compendium itself attempts to interrupt direct reconstruction. It omits
particular values, separates dependent diagrams, retains competing units, and
describes consequences more completely than construction. Those measures cannot
make it safe. Enough defensive knowledge can imply the attack it defends
against, and enough explanation can show a gifted reader where the editors have
cut.

## Refusal to read

Most people who recognize an authenticated Blackglass mark do not proceed to the
body text. Expedition practice favors recording the carrier, covering the
inscribed surface, and transmitting a description of the context before making
a legible copy. In many cultures reading a fragment aloud is taboo even when
silent possession is lawful.

The fear is not merely that a sentence will act upon the eye. Comprehension is
irreversible in an ordinary and terrible sense. A page can be closed, a file can
be destroyed, and a carving can be buried. A qualified reader cannot reliably
return to the state of not knowing which machines compose the geometry, which
ruin is an instrument, or which missing term can be derived.

Technical readers are therefore objects of pity, suspicion, and strategic value.
Religions call them burdened. Courts call them exposed or informed according to
jurisdiction. Cults call them chosen. The readers themselves often avoid any
collective name, because a named community is easier to recruit, monitor,
coerce, or make responsible for knowledge no one else will admit exists.

## Skeptical terror

Blackglass practice separates **confidence** from **consequence**.

The first question is evidentiary: how strongly do physical remains, independent
records, credible testimony, and reproducible observation support the account?
The second is operational: what is the largest plausible consequence if the
account is true?

A Grade E story can therefore require a planetary exclusion perimeter without
being promoted to fact. A Grade A event can remain physically unexplained. A
ruin can be acknowledged as artificial without identifying its builder. A
weapon correspondence can guide noncontact survey without proving common
lineage.

The result is sometimes called the **Blackglass posture** and more often
**skeptical terror**:

> Doubt the account. Respect the radius.

> Skepticism governs the claim. Terror governs the perimeter.

No existential claim should be confirmed by performing the event it predicts.
The inability to test safely is not evidence that the claim is true. It is also
not permission to behave as if the adverse result were recoverable.

## The second text in the margins

Blackglass margins are part of the collection, not decoration around it. They
contain translator decisions, variant readings, provenance, cross-references,
censor commands, legal restrictions, later discoveries, survivor objections,
and warnings that could not safely be promoted into the body.

In some witness families a marginal note is older than the current body text.
In others, yesterday's dissent becomes the next recension's principal account.
Notes answer one another across centuries. A later hand may know that an earlier
claim was false while preserving it because the error itself caused a war,
prohibition, discovery, or cult.

The official Codex often removes this apparatus to reduce ambiguity and
reconstructability. Blackglass retains it because the history of suppression is
evidence. That makes the margins both its greatest safeguard and its most
dangerous additional text.

### Recurring marks

The surviving symbols vary by medium. Current editors render several recurring
functions conventionally:

- a **hollow lozenge** marks text physically lost, unreadable, or absent from a
  witness where dependent text implies it once existed;
- a **black lozenge** marks deliberate excision, continuing restriction, or a
  passage the current editors possess but will not reproduce;
- an **obelus** marks a claim disputed but retained;
- a **double witness rule** marks agreement among sources assessed as genuinely
  independent;
- a **broken witness rule** marks apparent agreement probably inherited from one
  common copy;
- a **mirror mark** identifies a cross-reference removed from sanctioned Codex
  editions and restored in the Blackglass lineage; and
- an **unattributed hand** records an annotation whose linguistic or material
  layer can be bounded but whose author and institution cannot be named.

These are editorial functions, not proof that one symbol survived unchanged
across every medium and language. A stone notch, colored thread, damaged corner,
optical flag, and digital annotation can all become the same rendered mark in a
later recension.

Some cautions are preserved as sentences because no mark carries enough weight:

> This paragraph is false. Obey it anyway.

> Do not correct the unit. The corrected value is sufficient for construction.

> The official recension omits the following reference. We judge the omission
> prudent and restore it for evidentiary completeness.

> We tested only the noun.

## The Prior Hands and the Narrow Proof

The earliest recoverable editorial layer is obsessive about attribution, chain
of custody, disagreement, and the difference between an event and its
explanation. Yet its own compilers are unnamed.

The absence is too consistent to dismiss as ordinary decay. Several independent
witness families preserve grammatical positions, office titles, or attribution
marks from which personal or institutional names have been removed. The removal
therefore appears to predate the divergence of those families.

Modern scholarship calls the unknown compilers the **Prior Hands**. The term
does not establish their number, humanity, unity, period, or purpose. Even
*archivist* may be a flattering translation of a word that also meant witness,
jailer, examiner, confessor, or accomplice.

Beside one of the oldest lists of common omissions appears the most famous
Blackglass marginal claim:

> **The omissions name the Prior Hands. The demonstration is complete; this
> gutter is not.**

No demonstration follows. Later hands add:

> No demonstration accompanies any extant witness.

> The Sorrel recension doubled the outer margin. It remains blank.

> Nine independent reconstructions reached the same penultimate inference. None
> recorded the final name.

> The preceding statement is unsupported.

> The preceding statement is a warning.

The sequence ends, in a substantially later hand:

> Do not widen the margin.

The problem is known as the **Narrow Proof**, the **Gutter Claim**, or the
**Prior Hands Problem**. Attempts to complete it are prohibited in several
jurisdictions and religiously taboo in many more. Whether the missing conclusion
identifies people, a location, a technical lineage, or an operational concept is
unknown. So is whether any demonstration ever existed.

## The imp in the archive

Blackglass trouble has a characteristic unfairness. A fragment rarely gives a
reader the thing consciously sought. It gives the fact adjacent to it: the
answer that changes the question, the countermeasure that identifies a target,
the warning that proves an unnoticed apparatus is already present, or the
translation that makes a formerly harmless metaphor precise.

This behavior produces the widespread figure of the **Gutter Guest**, **Little
Reader**, or **margin imp**. The names describe a being that supplies the missing
word and then watches the reader complete the disaster. No common iconography or
single theology exists. In some stories it is malicious. In others it is
perfectly truthful and incapable of understanding why truth should be withheld.
Some traditions make it the ghost of a Prior Hand. Others make it the reader's
own cleverness seen from the outside.

The scholarly explanation is ordinary and strong. A vast fragmentary corpus
offers many opportunities for selective attention. People remember discoveries
followed by disaster and ignore harmless fragments. Qualified readers are most
likely to recognize dangerous implications. Secret institutions obscure the
base rate by reporting failures only after catastrophe.

The explanation has not ended the stories.

> A Blackglass answer is correct in the way that leaves the questioner wishing
> it were not.

## Cultural embeddings

Blackglass is feared differently because each culture maps it onto dangers it
already understands.

### Kharos

Kharosi courts and continuity schools treat a complete Blackglass apparatus as
both evidence and latent capability. Restricted readers work through separated
copies; no one archive is meant to contain every value, restored cross-reference,
and translation key. Courts distinguish protected belief and historical study
from the assembly of reconstructable knowledge, but possession of an innocent
fragment can still trigger aggressive quarantine.

Popular Kharosi speech calls an answer that proves its own danger **blackglass
correct**. To **leave a chair for the margin** means preserving one dissenter who
has not read the controlling text. Major memorial and religious traditions
oppose public recitation, partly because the dead should not be converted into
instruction for repeating their deaths.

### Avara

Avaran practice interprets hazardous comprehension through shared-environment
ethics. No private reader has the right to expose a habitat, atmosphere district,
or wet-cradle population merely by accepting personal risk. Translation is
treated as an environmental act because a successful rendering can change what
others nearby are capable of building or recognizing.

Some First Shelter observances leave one line deliberately untranslated. The
omission represents the principle that survival sometimes depends upon refusing
completion. Avaran custodial rooms have independent air, power, and outward-only
record paths. Their purpose is practical containment, though popular accounts
say Blackglass must never be allowed to “learn the air.”

### Odran

Odran's separated archives embody distrust of complete custody. Blackglass
fragments, translation keys, and technical concordances are kept on physically
independent pads with no shared foundation, life-support spine, clock, or sole
operator. No pad is supposed to know both how a passage is read and what device
it completes.

Odran workers call a dangerous adjacency discovered only by comparing isolated
records **the line between vaults**. The phrase is both professional warning and
folk omen. A common rule says that if two vaults independently request the same
missing page, neither request is fulfilled until a third world reviews the
reason.

### Valeci survivor communities

Valeci communities regard Blackglass with an especially bitter ambivalence. It
preserves testimony that official histories tried to separate, but it also
turns a murdered world into a technical lineage. Cohesion ledgers may quote
Blackglass evidence while refusing its filing numbers and omitting every
constructive parameter.

Many Valeci memorial readers will speak the names of the dead but not the full
name of the Rzez. They reject the claim that refusing to read is ignorance. In
their tradition, the refusal may be the last boundary the dead are still able to
enforce.

### Former Meridian cultures and the Concordant Temples

The [Concordant Temples](../canon/institutions/concordant-temples.md) provide the
most influential formal religious opposition. Their traditions disagree about
creation, personhood, and divine action, but broadly hold that knowledge which
can revoke another being's possibility of repair cannot become private
revelation. Blackglass may be preserved as a burdened witness; it must not be
worshipped as a source of permission.

Temple custodians historically sealed fragments, authorized reluctant defensive
readings, and prohibited the Severed God doctrine. Some orders cover reflective
surfaces during a Blackglass examination. Others leave a lamp, thermal path, or
open door as a sign that no inquiry may become a closed world. These practices
are related by later interpretation and do not imply one universal rite.

### Named Meridian inheritances

Older named cultures preserve narrower forms of the taboo.

At **Keth Aru**, a salt-and-obsidian city abandoned after subsidence, temple
inventories describe the black wedge that remembered the former names of stone,
metal, and bone. Later readers naturally associate the dark object with
Blackglass. The surviving inventories do not. Excavation remains prohibited, and
scholars dispute whether the resemblance preserved the collection's name,
created it locally, or is merely the consequence of describing two dark feared
objects with the same vocabulary.

The highland clans of **Orsa** treat completion as an imperial habit. Versions of
the Last Wedge account end before the clan mother's burial instructions, and
Orsan copyists refuse requests to harmonize the endings. A foreign scholar who
asks which version is correct is traditionally answered, “the one that leaves
the grave where it is.” Orsan Blackglass folklore therefore emphasizes that a
reader's demand for one final account may be the trespass that renews the
danger.

In the former port cultures associated with **Serevan** and the **Veyran
League**, a page without a book is a sailor's omen. Ceramic slips and repair
marks moved readily among ships, yards, insurers, rivals, and prize courts, so
ordinary transmission was common even when authorship was not. The Glass Fleet
made that ambiguity intolerable. A fragment found aboard a vessel may be a
warning, planted accusation, maintenance key, or evidence that standardized
repair has already made the fleet legible as one target. Serevan-derived custom
therefore records who brought a page aboard before anyone reads it.

The dynastic traditions once represented at **Kareth** treat archival
relationships as part of the dead. To extract a technical sequence from a
genealogy is not neutral translation but reuse of human remains. **Orel**
inspection culture is less theological and no less fearful: a Blackglass passage
about a bridge is not read on the bridge, because an engineer who understands
the warning may no longer be able to certify the structure without hearing a
third tone in every result.

Around the basin of **Meren**, local refusal is more complete. Nomadic accounts
call loose pages, detached shadows, and unowned names forms of the same bad
hospitality. The comparison is folklore, not evidence that Blackglass destroyed
the city or that a Rzez can act upon text. Investigators nevertheless do not use
the basin as a convenient place to discard confiscated fragments.

### Stations and route cultures

Habitat and route crews inherit language from several weapons. They speak of
open stars, uneven chairs, third tones, and pages without books. A returned
survey vessel carrying an unexplained fragment may be held outside ordinary
traffic even when its crew is healthy and its hull is clean.

Among long-route crews, **Blackglass at the threshold** means that an arrival has
brought home a question whose safe answer cannot yet be known. Some crews refuse
to read recovered writing aloud aboard a vessel. The practice is partly
superstition and partly sound information control in a closed environment with
no independent witness.

### Frontier and remote civilizations

Frontier societies often know the warning without possessing an authenticated
Compendium. Local equivalents describe the dark mirror, grief-book, unfinished
sentence, stone instruction, or truthful little visitor. Some may descend from
lost copies. Others may be independent responses to dangerous ruins or ordinary
misfortune.

The strongest cases come from remote human civilizations whose contact histories
do not explain the parallel. Their sayings do not reproduce one exact sentence,
but they share its structure:

> Where the dark mirror opens, grief learns the road.

> The black book does not enter an innocent house.

> Trouble walks behind the reader.

The recurrence proves neither supernatural transmission nor a common ancient
church. It raises the possibility that the warning travels with exploration
because the underlying encounters recur.

## Religious prohibition and Blackglass devotion

Most major religious authorities treat Blackglass as feared, spiritually
unclean, or borderline taboo even when their theology denies that the object is
supernaturally cursed. Common concerns include:

- understanding can impose risk upon people who never consented to the study;
- treating catastrophe as revelation grants authority to the instrument that
  caused it;
- complete recitation can become instruction;
- the desire to possess every answer is itself a moral failure; and
- preserving victims requires testimony, while converting their deaths into a
  usable design violates them again.

The taboo is rarely absolute. Religions that forbid lay reading may still train
custodians to recognize a fragment, preserve a warning, or advise an evacuation.
This creates an old accusation: every priesthood that condemns Blackglass keeps
a copy somewhere. Religious authorities answer that a sealed witness and a
sacred object are not the same thing.

[Blackglass devotional movements](../canon/institutions/blackglass-devotional-movements.md)
reverse the distinction. They worship the collection as an oracle, adversary,
test, wounded divinity, or instrument of selection. Some seek complete
understanding. Some venerate the merciful errors that prevent understanding.
Some believe the trouble following each fragment is proof that Blackglass has
noticed the reader.

These movements are not one conspiracy and do not share one physics. Rzez
liberation cults and the Houses of the Lower State may borrow Blackglass imagery
without becoming the same religion. The danger arises when devotion turns
translation, fragment hunting, public recitation, or technical completion into
a sacrament.

## Exterior witnesses to forbidden systems

Planetary exploration has repeatedly found evidence consistent with the use of
several weapons recorded in the Compendium. The strength of individual cases
varies from evocative morphology to artificial structures, ordered material
failure, surviving records, or mutually supporting instrument traces.

The editorial term **exterior witness** means evidence found outside the accepted
historical lineage of the relevant weapon. It does not mean that the weapon's
identity, builder, motive, or date has been proved.

The separated inner moon of Issa is the best-known Rzez correspondence. Other
surveys report incomplete radiant surfaces around dead installations,
anatomically organized ecologies without a supported local engineering history,
settlement remains whose independent records converge too cleanly, regions that
cannot be reduced to one survey history, and high-energy scars with suspicious
convergence geometry. Many accounts remain too weak, dangerous, or inaccessible
for close study.

The possibilities are all severe:

- known technologies are older than their accepted inventors;
- distant human societies independently rediscovered them;
- modern categories have collapsed unlike ancient phenomena into familiar
  names;
- an older human or nonhuman lineage distributed instruments, records, or
  warnings;
- ordinary nature repeatedly produces forms that technical cultures later turn
  into weapons; or
- some unidentified actor continues to use them.

No option establishes a common mechanism among the weapons. The recurrence may
belong to intelligence rather than physics: sufficiently capable civilizations
may repeatedly discover the same dangerous destinations by different roads.

The most pessimistic reading is also the simplest:

> The Compendium may not be a history of inventions. It may be a field guide to
> places where knowledge tends to arrive.

## Reader position

The represented reader has access to a Blackglass recension, not necessarily to
the official Codex or any physically complete archive. The medium, location,
legality, and reason for that access remain unresolved.

The reader should never be assured that the edition is harmless because it is
incomplete. Its omissions may be safeguards, wounds, invitations, or the exact
negative space from which a qualified mind can reconstruct what was removed.
Nor should the reader be told that every sentence is a trap. The Compendium
contains indispensable warnings, authentic testimony, ordinary history, false
claims, and knowledge that has saved lives.

That is why it survives. Destroying it would erase defenses and victims along
with instructions. Preserving it ensures that someone will eventually read too
far.

## Boundaries that remain unresolved

The following questions are intentionally not answered:

- whether the first Blackglass layer was a library, evidence locker,
  containment system, devotional work, or trap;
- whether the Prior Hands were human, contemporary with one another, or aware of
  the later Codex tradition;
- whether the name began with a material, a redaction practice, a mirror
  metaphor, or a convergence of all three;
- whether a complete recension has ever existed;
- whether the scattered-leaf and proximate-medium patterns have one cause;
- whether any page found in open space was placed for a particular discoverer;
- whether landscape correspondences are artifacts, natural analogues,
  misclassification, or evidence that history is radically incomplete;
- whether remote Blackglass myths descend from contact or recurrent discovery;
- whether the Narrow Proof had a solution; and
- whether trouble follows Blackglass, Blackglass follows trouble, or both follow
  something else.
